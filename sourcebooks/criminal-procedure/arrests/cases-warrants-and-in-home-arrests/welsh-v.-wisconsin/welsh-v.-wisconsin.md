# Welsh v. Wisconsin

466 U.S. 740 (1984)

### **Procedural Posture**

Edward Welsh was arrested in his home without a warrant for driving while intoxicated (DWI). He challenged the legality of the arrest, arguing it violated the Fourth Amendment. The trial court upheld the arrest, finding probable cause and exigent circumstances. The Wisconsin Court of Appeals reversed, but the Wisconsin Supreme Court reinstated the conviction. The U.S. Supreme Court granted certiorari to determine whether exigent circumstances justified a **warrantless nighttime home entry for a nonjailable offense**.

***

### **Facts (Highly Detailed Background)**

#### **Initial Driving Incident**

* On the **rainy evening of April 24, 1978**, shortly before 9:00 p.m.:
  * A private citizen, **Randy Jablonic**, observed a vehicle being driven in an **erratic and dangerous manner**.
  * The car:
    * Varied speeds unpredictably
    * **Weaved side-to-side across the roadway**
    * Ultimately **swerved off the road into a field**
* Importantly:
  * **No accident involving other persons or property occurred**
  * The danger was **potential rather than realized**

#### **Civilian Intervention**

*   Concerned that the driver might:

    * Return to the highway, or
    * Pose a continued danger

    Jablonic:

    * Positioned his truck **behind the vehicle to block its exit**
    * Asked another passerby to **call the police**

#### **Driver’s Conduct After Stopping**

* Before police arrived:
  * The driver (later identified as Welsh):
    * **Exited the vehicle**
    * Approached Jablonic
    * Requested a **ride home**
  * Jablonic declined and suggested waiting for assistance
* Welsh then:
  * **Ignored the suggestion**
  * **Walked away from the scene on foot**

#### **Police Investigation at the Scene**

* Officers arrived shortly thereafter and:
  * Interviewed Jablonic, who reported:
    * The driver appeared **“very inebriated or very sick”**
  * Checked the vehicle registration:
    * Identified the owner as **Edward Welsh**
  * Determined:
    * Welsh’s home was **nearby and within walking distance**

#### **Warrantless Entry into the Home**

* Without obtaining a warrant, officers:
  * Went directly to Welsh’s residence at approximately **9:00 p.m.**
  * Gained entry when:
    * Welsh’s **stepdaughter answered the door**
    * Police were **admitted into the home**
* Inside:
  * Officers proceeded upstairs to a bedroom
  * Found Welsh:
    * **Naked and lying in bed**

#### **Arrest and Evidence**

* Officers:
  * Arrested Welsh for **driving under the influence**
  * Transported him to the station
* At the station:
  * Welsh **refused a breathalyzer test**, triggering additional legal consequences under Wisconsin law

***

### **Statutory Context (Critical to the Case)**

* Under Wisconsin law at the time:
  * A **first-offense DWI** was:
    * **Noncriminal**
    * Punishable only by a **civil forfeiture (fine)**
    * **No jail time possible**
* This classification became central to the Court’s analysis of **government interest vs. privacy intrusion**

***

### **Issue**

Does the Fourth Amendment permit police to make a **warrantless, nighttime entry into a home** to arrest a suspect for a **nonjailable traffic offense**, based on alleged exigent circumstances?

***

### **Rule**

Warrantless home entries are **presumptively unreasonable** under the Fourth Amendment. Exigent circumstances may justify such entries, but:

* The government bears a **heavy burden**
* The **gravity of the underlying offense** is a critical factor
* Exigent circumstances are **rarely sufficient** when the offense is minor

***

### **Holding**

No. The warrantless home entry violated the Fourth Amendment. No exigent circumstances justified the intrusion given the **minor, nonjailable nature of the offense**.

***

### **Reasoning**

#### **1. Strong Protection of the Home**

* The Court reaffirmed the principle from _Payton v. New York_:
  * The home is at the **core of Fourth Amendment protection**
  * Warrantless entries are **presumptively unreasonable**

***

#### **2. Exigent Circumstances Doctrine (Narrow and Limited)**

* Exceptions to the warrant requirement are:
  * “**Few in number and carefully delineated**”
  * Subject to a **heavy burden of justification**

***

#### **3. Gravity of the Offense Matters**

* The Court introduced a key limiting principle:
  * The **seriousness of the offense** is central to evaluating exigency
* When the offense is **minor**:
  * The government’s interest in immediate arrest is **substantially diminished**
  * The presumption against warrantless entry becomes **very difficult to overcome**

***

#### **4. Application to the Facts**

**(a) No Hot Pursuit**

* The State argued “hot pursuit,” but:
  * There was **no immediate or continuous chase**
  * Police arrived after Welsh had already left the scene and gone home

**(b) No Ongoing Threat to Public Safety**

* Welsh:
  * Had **abandoned his car**
  * Was **no longer on the road**
* Therefore:
  * The danger to the public had effectively **ended**

**(c) Evidence Destruction (BAC Dissipation)**

* The State argued:
  * Blood alcohol evidence would dissipate over time
* The Court rejected this as sufficient:
  * Especially given that the offense was **noncriminal**
  * The State’s own classification showed its **limited interest in immediate arrest**

***

#### **5. Central Insight**

*   The Court emphasized:

    > When the government chooses to treat an offense as minor, it cannot then claim urgency sufficient to justify a major constitutional intrusion.

***

### **Disposition**

Judgment of the Wisconsin Supreme Court **vacated and remanded**. The arrest was unconstitutional.

***

### **Key Takeaways / Doctrinal Significance**

* Establishes that:
  * **Severity of the offense is a core factor** in exigent circumstances analysis
* Reinforces:
  * The **sanctity of the home** in Fourth Amendment doctrine
* Limits:
  * Use of **exigent circumstances** in minor offense cases
* Signals:
  * Courts will scrutinize **state policy choices** (e.g., classifying offenses as nonjailable)

***

### **Analytical Notes (Teaching-Oriented)**

#### **1. Structural Fourth Amendment Framework**

This case fits into a broader structure:

* **Step 1:** Is there a warrant? → No
* **Step 2:** Is there an exception? → Exigent circumstances
* **Step 3:** Is the exception justified? → Depends on:
  * urgency
  * danger
  * destructibility of evidence
  * **seriousness of offense (key here)**

***

#### **2. Normative Tension**

* The Court implicitly weighs:
  * **Individual privacy (home sanctity)**\
    vs.
  * **State interest in law enforcement**
* Here, privacy decisively wins because:
  * The offense is **minor**
  * The intrusion is **severe (home entry at night)**
